# Chapter 12: The Loyal Team

## The Weight of Devotion

The Universal Form session had recessed at five-twenty. The reconsideration motion was on the floor. Counsel was downstairs in a conference room pulling EDPB language. The board had scattered into the corridor and the executive suite kitchenette to call their husbands, their lawyers, their daughters. Dalton had given the room ninety minutes before the formal evening session, which would reconvene at seven for the brief and the substantive discussion.

Aaron had told Sarah to meet him in his office in five.

She arrived in three.

She came in without knocking. She didn't shut the door behind her until Aaron, watching her face, got up from behind his desk and shut it himself. She did not sit. She stood in the middle of the office, two arm's lengths from his desk, and put a manila folder on the glass surface between them. Her hands were shaking. She did not seem to be trying to hide it.

"This is everything I gave him," she said.

Aaron looked at the folder. He did not open it.

"Sarah."

"Open it."

He didn't.

"I authenticated the vulnerability from my own workstation," she said. "I emailed it from my personal account. I logged into the staging environment from a coffee shop in the Mission so it wouldn't show up on the office VPN. I knew exactly what I was doing. I want that on the record between us before anything else gets said."

She breathed in. She did not let it out smoothly.

"Damian's spreadsheet projected I'd net twelve million in cash after taxes if the acquisition closed at the price he'd modeled. Twelve million. I have one hundred and eighty-seven thousand dollars in savings. I have eighteen thousand in my checking account. My mother is in subsidized housing in Worcester and the waitlist for the better facility is four years and the better facility is six thousand a month. I sat at my desk and I did the math on what twelve million would mean and I gave him the vulnerability. That's what happened. I want to say it out loud once."

Aaron felt the floor of his chest drop. He kept his face still.

"Sit down, Sarah."

"I don't want to sit."

"Sit."

She sat. She was crying without making any sound. He had known her for fourteen years and he had seen her cry exactly twice—once at MIT in the basement, once at her father's memorial. This was the third.

He came around the desk and sat in the chair next to hers, not across from her. He did not touch her. He waited.

"Talk to me," he said.

"I keep thinking about him," she said. "My dad. I keep thinking that the whole reason we wrote this thing in the first place was because of him, and somehow I still walked it into a room that was going to grind it up and sell it for parts. I keep thinking how I can possibly hold both of those things in the same head."

"Tell me how."

"I don't have an answer that lets me keep my self-respect. I have an answer about being afraid." She pressed the heel of her hand into her eye, hard. "When the seventy-two-hour pre-auth ran out and I drove down from Boston and he was already dead—" Her voice cracked. She made herself continue. "The thing I learned that weekend wasn't that systems kill people. I'd already known that. The thing I learned was that systems kill people and then the next month comes anyway and you still have rent. And ever since then there has been a part of me that is afraid of being the person who needs the system to work. And Damian gave me a number that meant I never had to be that person."

Aaron did not speak.

"I told myself I was hedging the company," she said. "That if Damian was going to win anyway, at least my mother could have the better facility. I told myself a lot of things." She looked at him directly for the first time. "I didn't actually believe any of them. I knew what I was doing. I want that on the record between us."

"Okay."

"Okay?"

"Okay. It's on the record. What now."

She looked at him. She had not expected that.

"I want to fix the vulnerability," she said. "I want to fix it tonight. I want to be in the room when Elena verifies the patch. I want my name on the commit. I want it in the audit log. I want every shareholder, when this is over, to be able to look at the audit trail and see exactly when the authentication flaw closed and whose hands closed it."

"Done."

"And after the vote—whichever way it goes—I want you to tell the board what I did. Not me. You. From the chair."

"Sarah."

"I can't say it. I tried in the hallway. I won't get the words out without breaking. If you say it for me, they'll believe it more, because it'll cost you to say it. That's the only way they'll know I'm not negotiating my way back into the room."

"Okay."

"And one more thing."

"Yeah."

"If we lose the vote. If Damian wins anyway." She drew a hard breath. "I'll go with the company into the acquisition. I'll be inside. I'll document everything they do and I will give it to whatever regulator wants it. I will burn what's left of my career on the inside instead of running."

"You don't have to—"

"I want to. I owe it to him. I should have done it on day one when Damian first reached out to me. Instead I priced my mother's care against the company. I will not do that twice."

She was, Aaron saw, completely composed now in the way that people are composed in the minute after something has actually broken in them. She was not performing strength. She had simply run out of things to be afraid of.

He nodded. He did not trust himself to say anything else for a moment.

"Seven o'clock," he said finally. "Evening session. Counsel's brief. Reconsideration motion."

"I'll be there."

"At your seat."

"At my seat."

"Patch the auth flaw first."

"Already paged Elena."

She stood up. At the door she paused, her hand on the knob.

"Aaron."

"Yeah."

"For what it's worth. I'm sorry."

"I know."

She went out.

Aaron sat for a long moment with the manila folder on the glass. He still hadn't opened it. He did not need to. He picked it up and put it in the wall safe behind the Kahlo print without looking at the contents.

Chris was outside the office when he opened the door.

"How long were you there?" Aaron asked.

"Long enough. Not the part that was hers."

"Good."

"Walk with me. Dalton wants you in the corridor at six forty-five to look him in the eye before the session reconvenes. He doesn't want it written down anywhere that he wanted to do that, but he wants to do that. So at six forty-five you'll be in the corridor outside the boardroom and he'll happen to be there."

Aaron nodded. He looked at his watch. It was a quarter past six.

"Elena," he said.

"In her bay. She's already pulling the auth patch. She told Sarah to use her keys. Sarah is in there now. They're side by side at Elena's monitor."

Aaron exhaled.

"Tell me something about Elena I don't know," he said. "Not to use. Just to know."

Chris considered. They were walking now, slowly, through the empty executive suite on the forty-seventh floor.

"Her mother was a cardiac surgeon at home," Chris said. "Trained in Dhaka. After Elena's brother died at thirteen of the undiagnosed congenital defect, the family emigrated. Elena was eight. They went to Des Moines. The mother's credentials didn't carry. She worked three jobs and tutored Elena at the kitchen table at night. Elena learned to code at ten on a donated Pentium their church had in a closet."

"Why has she never told me any of this."

"Because you never asked her about her brother, and the brother is the door to the rest of it. She doesn't lead with the brother. Nobody leads with the brother. You walk past the door for five years and one day at two in the morning you sit down next to her and she opens it. That's all."

Aaron stopped walking. He pressed the heel of his hand into his eye for a moment, the same gesture Sarah had used in his office twenty minutes earlier. He recognized it as he did it. He did not stop.

"All right," he said.

"All right."

They kept walking.

---

The boardroom at seven was a different room. Counsel had distributed a six-page brief on EDPB exposure, the standstill agreement, and the procedural mechanics of substituting the API pivot for the underlying acquisition motion. The board members had read it. The room smelled faintly of coffee and printer toner and the day's accumulated tension.

Dalton called the session to order at three minutes past seven.

"We have on the floor a motion to reconsider the original substantive question," Dalton said, "and to substitute in its place the API subscription pivot as proposed by the Chief Executive Officer. Before we move to debate on the substitution itself, I'm going to give the Chief Executive Officer ten minutes. He's asked for the floor. The chair grants the floor."

Aaron stood.

He looked at Sarah first. She did not look away.

"Two things," he said. "The first is procedural and I want to put it on the record before the substantive discussion. At three forty-seven this morning, an authentication vulnerability in the Ethos staging environment was identified and logged. The vulnerability had been previously documented by a member of this company and that documentation had been shared, prior to this morning, with a representative of Harwell Capital. I want the board to know that the vulnerability was patched at six twelve this evening by the original engineer who documented it, working with our chief architect Elena. The audit log will reflect that. The vulnerability was disclosed by the engineer to me, in writing, within the past hour. The engineer has asked me to read this disclosure into the record."

The room had gone completely still. Sarah was very pale. Her hands were folded in her lap, knuckles white.

Aaron continued. He named what had happened. He named whose hands had done it and whose hands had closed it. He did not editorialize. He read the disclosure flat, the way Sarah had asked. When he was finished, he looked at her once and she gave him a single nod and looked back down at the table.

"I am asking the board, in the substantive discussion that follows, not to act on this disclosure. Not yet. We have an acquisition motion in front of us, and a strategic alternative, and a regulatory exposure on the buyer's side that is material. The disclosure I just read is a fourth piece of information. It belongs in the record. It does not belong, today, in the calculation of which strategic direction this company takes. Whatever the board decides about the disclosure is a matter for the board's audit and ethics committee after the strategic direction has been resolved. I am asking that those two questions stay separated."

He paused. Dalton's hands were folded on the table. Dalton had not moved.

"The second thing," Aaron said. "The numbers in this brief are good. They're conservative. Counsel's overlay on the regulatory carrying cost is honest. You've all read it. I'm not going to repeat it. I want to talk about something Damian Harwell cannot put on a slide.

"You can buy capital. You can buy access. You can buy a hedge fund's worth of leverage and a year's worth of legal hours. You cannot buy what makes this company work. What makes it work is that the engineers in this building, every one of them, are working on a problem that has a face. Sarah's father waiting for a cardiology authorization. My sister, eleven years old, three days before her birthday, with a lung infection the system flagged as a routine cough until it wasn't. Elena's brother in Dhaka, thirteen, with a heart defect that an interconnected system would have caught at six.

"Those are not stories the marketing team made up. Those are the reasons the people in our basement-floor dev pit will work a seventy-two-hour sprint on a logic error because a hospital in Mumbai is depending on the API to be up at three in the morning. You cannot manufacture that. You cannot recruit for it. You cannot bonus it into existence. It is either there or it is not. Damian's playbook works on companies where it is not. It will not work on this one. It will hollow the engine out trying."

He looked at Marcus. Marcus did not look away.

"Marcus, the first time we sat across a table from each other in Cambridge, you told me you'd backed a hundred founders with good ideas and invested in three. You said you could smell whether the founder had the problem in their chest or was just trying to get rich. You said the ones who had it in their chest were the only ones who'd survive when it got hard. It got hard, Marcus. It got very hard. The question on the floor is whether you smell what you smelled then or whether you have, in the last forty-eight hours, started to smell something else."

Marcus's eyes were wet. He did not blink.

"Patricia," Aaron said, "you know what venture capital is supposed to be. You haven't forgotten. The brief in front of you is good. The pivot pencils. It pencils harder than the buyout, on any honest five-year horizon, and counsel has just told you the buyout pencils worse than the buyer is letting on. The numbers are not the problem. The problem is the courage. I'm asking you for the courage."

"William," Aaron said. "Your daughter's hospital is in this database. You've already done the math on what the extraction model does to her patients. You said it yourself this morning."

He let it sit.

"That's the floor," Aaron said. He sat down.

Damian did not stand to respond. He had been instructed by his counsel, Aaron could see, to stay silent through this portion. He sat with his hands folded on a yellow legal pad. He did not look at Aaron. He looked at Dalton.

Dalton looked at the brief in front of him for a long moment. Then he looked up at the room.

"The motion before the board is to reconsider the underlying substantive question and to substitute the API pivot as the operative proposal," Dalton said. "Substantive debate is open. Reminder to the room that no final vote on the substantive question is being taken tonight. Tonight is the substitution motion and the procedural framework for the substantive vote. Substantive vote will be scheduled within the week, pending counsel's regulatory brief and a clean draft of the pivot resolution. The chair recognizes Mrs. Oakes."

Patricia Oakes spoke. Then William Chen. Then Elizabeth from Accel. Then Marcus, more briefly than Aaron had expected, in a voice Aaron had not heard him use in a boardroom in years. Damian's counsel raised three procedural objections; two were overruled; the third triggered a five-minute consultation with outside counsel in the corridor.

When they came back, Dalton called the question on the substitution motion.

"All in favor of placing the API pivot proposal in operative position for the substantive vote, displacing the original acquisition motion pending the scheduled session within the week."

Hands went up. Sarah's first—she did not hesitate, did not look at anyone. Marcus's. Elizabeth's. Patricia's. William's. Thomas Brennan's. David from Tiger Global, after a beat. Dalton's, last.

"Opposed."

No hands.

"Abstentions."

None.

"The substitution motion carries. The substantive vote is scheduled for Thursday at ten a.m. in this room. Mr. Harwell, your offer remains on the table; you may, if you choose, present an amended offer at the substantive session. Standstill remains in force. We are adjourned until Thursday."

Damian's counsel began packing up the legal pads.

Aaron looked at Sarah. She looked back. She did not smile. Neither did he.

The substitution had carried. The substantive vote had not. The fight had not ended. It had only changed shape.

Outside the windows, the bay had gone fully dark. The boardroom lights came up to their evening level. The reconsideration was on the calendar. The room exhaled, once, together, and then began to gather its papers.
