# Chapter 2: The Architect's View

## The Drive to the Hills

The city blurred past the tinted windows of the sleek, black electric sedan. Rain had started to fall, smearing the neon lights of the metropolis into long, bleeding streaks of red and gold. The streets were nearly empty at this hour—just the occasional taxi, its rooftop light glowing like a single watchful eye, and the ghostly silhouettes of late-shift workers waiting at bus stops under the anemic glow of sodium lamps. The city looked like it was bleeding out. Or maybe like it was dreaming. At this hour, between the close of evening and the arrival of morning, the distinction seemed thin.

Aaron sat in the passenger seat, staring blankly at the hypnotic rhythm of the wipers. Each sweep cleared the glass for a fraction of a second before the rain reasserted itself, and in that brief clarity, the city appeared sharp and merciless—all hard edges and vertical ambition—before dissolving again into an impressionist blur. It felt like a metaphor for his life. Moments of clarity drowning in chaos. Or perhaps it was the inverse: moments of illusion dissolving to reveal a clearer, harsher truth underneath.

The car itself was a marvel of silent engineering. Aaron recognized it as one of the latest luxury electric vehicles—probably a Tesla Model S Plaid or something comparable, the kind of car that cost more than most people's houses and advertised that cost through its pristine finish and barely perceptible movement. There was something fitting about it: a vehicle of extraordinary power that operated almost silently, that could accelerate from zero to sixty in less than three seconds, that seemed to glide rather than drive. It was like traveling in a dream of motion, the displacement occurring without the usual sensory markers of acceleration.

Chris navigated the late-night traffic with effortless precision, his hands resting lightly on the steering wheel at ten and two, steering them away from the financial district and toward the quiet, winding roads of the hills overlooking the bay. He drove the way he did everything—with an economy of motion that suggested deep reserves held in check, as if the car were merely an extension of his will and the road a conversation he had memorized long ago. His movements were never rushed, never desperate, never reactive. They were anticipatory. He seemed to know what the car would do before it did it, as if he existed slightly ahead of normal time.

For twenty minutes, neither man spoke. The hum of the electric motor was the only sound, a low, continuous vibration that Aaron felt more in his bones than heard with his ears. It was the sound of potential energy, of power restrained, and it stood in stark contrast to the chaos screaming inside his skull. Aaron found his thoughts cycling through the same anxious loops: the proxy materials, the secret negotiations, the fundamental betrayal of his closest allies. His mind was a machine that couldn't stop running, that couldn't find the off switch.

He found himself studying Chris's profile in the dashboard's ambient light. The man was an enigma that five years of close proximity had failed to solve. Aaron had Googled him, of course—obsessively, in the early days, when a stranger writes you a half-million-dollar check based on a forty-minute car ride, you investigate. But the digital footprint of Christopher Anand was remarkably shallow. A Stanford degree in applied mathematics from 1997—or was it 1987? The university website listed both dates at different points in its archive; when Aaron tried to verify, the older records seemed to have been digitally altered. A brief stint at a think tank in Geneva that specialized in global health economics, a think tank that seemed to have been dissolved in 2010 but whose publications still existed and still influenced major health organizations. A handful of academic papers on complex adaptive systems that had been cited thousands of times but never seemed to generate the kind of public profile such work deserved. The papers were dense, brilliant, almost prophetic—they predicted the dynamics of financial crises before the 2008 collapse, described epidemiological patterns that wouldn't emerge until COVID, modeled the bifurcation of technological systems in ways that only now, fifteen years later, were being validated by experience.

And then—silence. A decade of near-invisibility before he surfaced in San Francisco, attending pitch events and quietly funding founders that the mainstream venture world overlooked. Founders who were often rejected by others, founders with unconventional visions, founders driven by mission rather than exit strategy. And remarkably, many of them succeeded. Not all, but a surprisingly high percentage. Chris seemed to have an almost prescient ability to identify which wild visions would eventually reshape markets.

"Who are you, Chris?" Aaron had asked him once, two years into their partnership, over late-night whiskey in Aaron's apartment. They'd been celebrating the Series A close, and Aaron had been drunk enough to push past the polite boundaries that normally constrained their conversations. "I mean, really. Where did you come from? What are you doing in my life?"

Chris had swirled his glass, watching the amber liquid catch the light, watching the way the alcohol seemed to have depth and dimension when you looked at it properly. "I am the question you just asked," he had replied, with the kind of smile that was either profound or evasive, depending on your tolerance for mystery. "And the answer changes depending on who is asking."

"That's not an answer," Aaron had pressed, feeling the boldness of whiskey and success.

"Isn't it?" Chris had replied. "You asked me who I am. The most honest answer is that I'm the mirror you're looking into. You see in me what you need to see. Another founder sees something different. Another investor sees something else entirely. We're all looking at the same person and seeing a different reflection."

"But you must have come from somewhere. You must have a past."

"I have many pasts," Chris had said. "Or perhaps I'm from the future, looking backward. Or perhaps I exist only in the present moment, with neither past nor future. Does it matter? What matters is whether I'm useful to you right now."

Aaron had laughed it off then, chalking it up to philosophical posturing and expensive whiskey. Tonight, the question felt less academic. Tonight, Aaron was beginning to suspect that Chris's evasiveness wasn't coyness but honesty—that he genuinely existed outside the normal categories of biography and history.

---

Finally, Chris broke the silence. His voice was calm, contrasting sharply with the chaotic storm raging in Aaron's mind.

"You speak of destroying relationships, Aaron. You speak of 'your' company, 'your' friends, and the tragedy of tearing it all down. But tell me—what exactly is Ethos Tech?"

Aaron frowned, irritated by the philosophical detour. The clock on the dashboard read 1:17 AM. In less than eight hours, the boardroom doors would lock, and his life would be decided by a vote. This was not the time for Socratic questioning.

"You know what it is," he said, his voice flat with exhaustion. "It's five years of my life. It's the algorithms Sarah and I built. It's the mission to keep health data out of the hands of predators like Damian."

"That is what it *does*," Chris corrected gently, as if redirecting a promising student who had given a technically accurate but fundamentally incomplete answer. "But what *is* it? Strip away the function. What remains? Is it the glass tower we just left? Is it the servers humming in the Nevada desert? Is it the stock ticker symbol that financial analysts stare at like a holy text?"

"It's the people," Aaron insisted, though his voice lacked conviction. The people had just voted to gut the mission. The people had chosen Damian's spreadsheet over Priya's promise.

"The people?" Chris chuckled softly—not unkindly, but with the affectionate exasperation of a man watching someone cling to an answer they already know is wrong. "The same people who just voted to gut the mission for a payout? No. Ethos Tech is none of those things. Those are just the temporary shells. The vessels. The packaging."

---

Chris steered the car into a scenic overlook that Aaron had driven past a hundred times but never actually stopped at. The kind of place tourists photographed at sunset and locals ignored. It was accessed via a small, unmarked pullout off the main road, the kind of place that existed in the margins of official geography. Most people missed it entirely. But Chris, naturally, navigated directly toward it as if he'd been there countless times.

Tonight, though, with the rain thinning to a fine mist and the city sprawling below them like a massive, glowing circuit board, the view was arresting. The bay was visible in the distance, a dark mirror reflecting the city lights with an inverted luminescence. Oakland spread out below them like a circuit board, San Francisco beyond it like a crystalline fortress of ambition, and above it all, the night sky beginning to show the first hints of stars as the rain cleared.

Chris put the car in park but left the system running, the quiet hum persisting like a mechanical heartbeat. The battery percentage display showed 85 percent charge remaining. They could have run it for hours without draining the battery completely. Somewhere in the middle distance, Aaron could hear the faint sound of the bay's waters, though they were far too high and far too distant for the sound to be physically real. Perhaps it was memory, or perhaps it was something else—some kind of atmospheric resonance that only existed in the space between sleeping and waking.

"Listen to me carefully," Chris said, turning to face Aaron. The ambient light from the dashboard cast long shadows across his face, deepening the hollows of his cheeks, sharpening the line of his jaw, making him look older, ancient even—or perhaps not older, but timeless, as if the face were a window and behind it lay not decades but centuries of accumulated understanding.

"You are confusing the vehicle with the destination. You are confusing the body with the soul."

Aaron rubbed his temples, pressing his fingers into the tension that had taken up permanent residence above his eyebrows. "Chris, this isn't a university philosophy seminar. I have a board vote in eight hours that will determine the fate of a billion-dollar company."

"And that is exactly why you need to understand this now," Chris replied, his tone hardening just enough to demand attention—not with the aggressive sharpness of a Damian, but with the precise firmness of a surgeon who knows the patient is running out of time for anesthetic. "You are paralyzed because you think you are killing something real. You think destroying the current structure of the board, or firing your old mentors, means destroying the essence of what you built."

He turned in his seat and pointed through the rain-streaked windshield at the glowing city below.

"Look at those lights. Every one of them represents a node of human ambition. Companies rise and fall down there every day—some of them literally built in the last few months, others that have been running for decades. Startups burn bright and die—I have watched it happen a hundred times. A thousand times, across different cities, different centuries of commerce. In Renaissance Florence, in Victorian London, in Silicon Valley in the 1990s and 2000s and now. A young person with fire in their belly builds something extraordinary—something they believe will matter, something born from genuine passion and vision. And then the structure around it—the investors who smell profit, the board that demands returns, the market that offers validation only to those who optimize for extraction—all of that machinery tries to consume the fire for fuel."

Aaron stared out the window, listening despite his resistance. He could see the pattern Chris was describing—could see it in Damian's acquisition strategy, could see it in the way Marcus had rationalized his betrayal. The fire of vision meeting the machinery of extraction. And the machinery usually won.

Somewhere down there, Aaron knew, a thousand other entrepreneurs were probably working late nights, driven by vision and mission, unaware of how thin the line was between creation and exploitation. How easily the purity of purpose could be corrupted by the seductive logic of scale. How quickly the desire to change the world could be transformed into the desire to own the world.

"The structures are impermanent," Chris continued. "They are born, they exist for a time, and they dissolve. Consider the Dutch East India Company. The largest corporation that ever existed. It controlled spice routes, shipping lanes, the destiny of continents. Wealth that dwarfed the GDPs of nations. Where is it now?"

"A museum exhibit in Amsterdam," Aaron said.

"A footnote. The structure dissolved." Chris let the rain on the windshield speak for a moment. "But the impulse it embodied—to connect distant civilizations through trade, to move goods and knowledge across oceans—that did not die with the company. It migrated. It became the colonial empires. Then the shipping conglomerates. Then the fiber-optic cables under the Atlantic carrying data at the speed of light. The structure died a dozen deaths. The vision was immortal."

Chris turned, his dark eyes locking onto Aaron's with an intensity that made the air in the car feel charged.

"The vision of democratized knowledge—your sister's unfinished algorithm, the drive to use technology to heal rather than exploit—that is eternal, Aaron. It cannot be bought by Damian. It cannot be voted out by Sarah or Marcus. It cannot be killed by a hostile takeover or a stock crash. It exists independently of Ethos Tech, just as the vision of global connectivity exists independently of any company that has ever carried it."

---

Aaron was quiet for a long time. The rain had almost stopped, leaving the city below crystalline and sharp, the lights reflecting off wet surfaces so that the entire metropolis seemed to shimmer, as if the boundary between the real city and its reflection had dissolved.

He thought about Priya. Not the hospital Priya, the one wrapped in tubes and monitors. The living Priya. The one who used to sit on the fire escape of their Queens apartment on summer evenings, her legs dangling between the iron railings, reading Harry Potter books she had borrowed from the school library because their mother couldn't afford to buy them. Priya, who had announced at age nine, with the absolute certainty that only children possess, that she was going to be a doctor and fix everyone in their neighborhood.

The *idea* of healing. The *vision* of a world where a child's zip code didn't determine her survival. That idea hadn't died with Priya. It had migrated to Aaron. And if Ethos Tech died tomorrow, it would migrate again—to another founder, another codebase, another desperate, brilliant kid in another basement.

But that thought, rather than comforting him, made him angry.

"So, what?" Aaron countered, a spark of fire returning to his voice. "I just let Damian have it? If the idea is eternal, then he can't kill it either, right? I'll just go start over. Ethos 2.0. New investors. Clean slate."

"No," Chris said sharply. The word cracked through the car like a whip.

Aaron recoiled. In five years, he had never heard Chris raise his voice. The man's power lay precisely in his quietness—a calm so deep it made other people's noise seem absurd. This sudden shift in register was like watching the surface of a still lake suddenly crack.

"Excuse me?"

"That is cowardice disguised as enlightenment," Chris said, his eyes locking onto Aaron's with a ferocity that was entirely new. "You are retreating, Aaron. You are using the excuse of 'preserving relationships' and 'starting over' to avoid the ugly, difficult duty right in front of you."

The word hit like a physical blow. *Cowardice.* Aaron had been called many things—naive, idealistic, reckless, even arrogant—but never a coward. The accusation landed in the deepest part of his pride, the part that had driven him out of Queens and into MIT, the part that had kept him coding through pneumonia and presenting through panic attacks.

"I am not a coward," Aaron said, his jaw tightening.

"Then prove it," Chris replied, and his voice was no longer gentle. It carried the uncompromising edge of a man who had earned the right to deliver hard truths by the sheer depth of his investment in the person receiving them. "You are a CEO, Aaron. A guardian. The eternal vision of Priya's algorithm—the mission to democratize knowledge and health—is currently housed within the temporary structure of Ethos Tech. That is the vessel. And right now, thieves are breaking into the house."

He leaned closer, and the shadows on his face seemed to deepen, as if the darkness were not falling on him but emanating from somewhere behind his eyes.

"You do not abandon the house because the thieves are your old friends. You do not walk away because fighting them will be painful. You lock the doors. You call the authorities. You defend the vessel, because the vessel is all you have *right now* to carry the vision forward. If you let it fall without a fight, you are not honoring the eternal idea. You are abandoning it."

---

"But they are my friends!" Aaron protested, and the word came out ragged, torn. *Friends.* The word felt inadequate for what Sarah and Marcus had been to him. Sarah, who had held his hand through his first panic attack at a board meeting. Marcus, who had called him at midnight when Aaron's mother was diagnosed with diabetes and offered to pay for the best endocrinologist in the city. These were not LinkedIn connections. These were the people who had seen him cry.

"They *were* your friends when they aligned with the vision," Chris corrected, and his voice had softened again—not to gentleness, but to precision. "The friendship was authentic, Aaron. I am not asking you to deny that. What I am telling you is that the alignment has broken. They have allowed the disease of extraction and short-term profit to infect their judgment. They have looked at Priya's legacy and seen a line item."

Chris paused, and when he spoke again, there was a sadness in his voice that surprised Aaron—a deep, old sadness, as if Chris were mourning not just this specific betrayal but a pattern of betrayal he had witnessed too many times to count.

"Your duty is not to Sarah the college roommate, or Marcus the early investor. Your duty is to the vision you swore to protect. If protecting that vision requires removing them from the structure, then that is your duty. It is painful. It is necessary. And you must do it without hatred."

Chris turned back to the steering wheel, looking out over the city. The mist had cleared entirely now, and the lights below were hard and precise, each one a discrete point of human activity, each one unaware of the others.

"Consider the code you write," Chris murmured, his voice dropping to the contemplative register that Aaron had come to associate with Chris's most important insights. "When a module becomes corrupted—when a function starts returning garbage values that threaten the integrity of the entire system—what do you do? Do you leave it because you spent weeks writing it? Do you mourn the deletion of those lines of code?"

Aaron knew the answer before he said it. It was the first principle of software engineering, the lesson every programmer learned before they learned anything else: sentiment has no place in debugging.

"No," Aaron admitted. "You isolate it. You rewrite it. Or you delete it."

"Exactly. You don't hate the corrupted module. You don't blame it for being corrupted. You simply recognize that its current state is incompatible with the system's purpose, and you act accordingly." Chris's reflection in the windshield nodded slowly, as if confirming something to himself. "The corporate battlefield is no different. Sarah, Marcus, Mr. Dalton—they have become corrupted modules in this specific system. Not corrupted as people, perhaps—their personal virtues may remain intact in other contexts. But in *this* system, in *this* moment, their values are producing outputs that threaten the integrity of the mission."

"To save the system's purpose," Chris concluded, "you must excise them. Tomorrow morning, you must walk into that room not as Aaron the friend, but as the architect of the system. You must cut away the rot."

He paused, his hands steady on the wheel despite the fact that the car had slowed almost to a stop, moving at the pace of someone engaged in profound conversation rather than transportation.

"I understand this is difficult for you. I understand that every fiber of your being is resisting this understanding. You have internalized the modern mythology that business is separate from ethics, that profit is separate from purpose, that you can have corporate relationships divorced from human relationships. But this separation is an illusion, Aaron. A dangerous one."

Chris pulled the car slightly to the shoulder, putting it in park. He turned to look directly at Aaron, and in the dim light, his face seemed to contain depths that hadn't been there before—or perhaps had always been there and Aaron was only now beginning to perceive them.

"Let me ask you something. Why do you think Damian chose this specific moment? Why did he not move against you two months ago, or six months from now? Why did he time the hostile takeover to coincide with this particular board meeting?"

Aaron had been wondering the same thing. "Because he felt the momentum shifting. Because the data vulnerabilities had been documented. Because Marcus was ready to turn. Because—"

"Because," Chris interrupted gently, "he sensed a weakness in your conviction. He sensed that you were beginning to doubt the mission. He sensed that you were tired, burned out, questioning whether any of this mattered. The predator can smell exhaustion. The predator can sense when the prey is no longer willing to fight. And when that sensing occurs, the predator strikes."

Chris started the car again, accelerating back onto the road, moving them back toward the city, back toward Ethos Tech, back toward the confrontation that waited.

"But more importantly, Aaron, Damian moved when he did because he understands something that most founders never learn: the window between creation and extraction closes very quickly. Once a company has scaled beyond a certain point, once it has attracted enough capital and institutional attention, the forces of extraction become irresistible. The board gets stacked with people who have no relationship to the original vision. The investors demand returns. The market rewards only those companies that maximize shareholder value. And suddenly, the founder finds himself surrounded by people who all agree that the mission was beautiful but ultimately impractical."

The car descended the hills, moving back toward the financial district, back toward the towers of glass and steel where the battle would be fought.

---

Aaron felt a strange, cold clarity beginning to pierce through his panic. It was like the moment in a fever when the temperature breaks—the sweat suddenly cold on the skin, the room snapping into focus after hours of delirium. The emotional fog was lifting, not because the emotions had disappeared, but because they had been contextualized. They were no longer the whole of reality; they were a layer of reality, legitimate and real, but not the deepest layer.

Beneath the grief of losing Sarah's friendship, there was the bedrock of Priya's promise.

Beneath the guilt of weaponizing Marcus's financial vulnerabilities, there was the structural duty of the system builder.

Beneath the fear of public humiliation, there was the cold, clear recognition that the humiliation was temporary and the mission was not.

"It will be brutal," Aaron whispered. The words were not a complaint. They were a measurement. He was calculating the cost with the dispassionate precision of an engineer assessing structural load.

"Yes," Chris agreed without hesitation. His voice held no false comfort, no motivational gloss. "It will be. The press will crucify you. They will run stories about the 'boy genius who lost his mind.' The financial analysts will call you erratic and downgrade the stock. The Twitterverse will generate memes of your face photoshopped onto sinking ships. You will lose people you care about—not just from the board, but from the broader ecosystem. People who were waiting to see which way the wind blew before committing to your side."

Chris started the car again, putting it in drive. The engine—silent, electric, powerful—hummed to life beneath them.

"But you must fight, Aaron. Not out of anger. Not out of revenge against Damian. Not out of the ego-driven desire to 'win' and prove yourself right. But because it is your designated duty to protect the integrity of the system you built. You built this house. You filled it with the eternal vision. The thieves are inside. You fight because the house is not yours to surrender."

The car began to roll down the winding hillside road, the city lights growing larger and brighter as they descended back toward the financial district. The descent was gradual, the elevation dropping in measured increments, the landscape shifting from the austere beauty of the hills to the dense sprawl of urban development. Chris drove with the same effortless precision he brought to everything—the car responding to his inputs as though they were one integrated organism rather than separate entities.

Aaron felt the shift in his understanding. The fear had dissolved, replaced by something harder and clearer: the recognition that the choice wasn't actually difficult. He either fought or he didn't. He either protected what he'd built or he let it be destroyed. There was no middle ground. There was no compromise that would preserve the mission while also preserving his relationships with Sarah and Marcus and Damian. The systems were incompatible. The design required choosing.

The clock on the dashboard read 1:42 AM.

"You cannot be attached to the result," Chris added, his eyes on the road, his voice carrying the cadence of a lesson he had taught before—perhaps many times, to many people, across many dark nights. "You might launch your defense tomorrow and still lose the vote. The stock might tank. You might be fired. Damian might take the company and sell it for parts. All of that is possible, and all of it is entirely out of your control."

Aaron looked at him, genuinely confused. The cold clarity was still there, but this new statement seemed to undermine it. "Then why fight so hard if I might lose anyway? If the outcome doesn't matter, why not just... coast?"

"Because the fight itself is the requirement," Chris answered, and the words carried a profound certainty that seemed to originate not from Chris's personal opinion but from somewhere deeper—from the structure of reality itself, as if he were not expressing a philosophy but reporting a natural law.

"You have no right to the fruits of your labor, Aaron. You only have the right to the labor itself. You fight because it is the right thing to do, regardless of whether you win or lose. You act not for the reward, but because the action is necessary. You protect the house not because you are guaranteed to save it, but because protection is your duty, and duty is performed without conditions."

The city was close now. Aaron could see the glass tower of Ethos Tech, its upper floors still lit, a beacon in the fog. Somewhere in that building, the defense documents sat on his desk, waiting. The SEC filings documenting Damian's violations. The shell company analysis showing the web of coordinated acquisitions. The leverage points where Sarah's security vulnerabilities could be exposed. The bank statements showing Marcus's short position. The weapons he had built and abandoned in his despair.

He closed his eyes. The panic in his chest was gone, replaced by a heavy, resolute stillness—not the stillness of resignation, but the stillness of decision. The stillness that precedes action. The moment a chess player's hand hovers over the piece, and the board is already rewritten in his mind. The moment before the opening move commits you to the sequence that will determine the game.

He thought about the legal files, the leveraged positions, the PR strategy he had prepared and abandoned. He thought about the scorched-earth campaign he had been too afraid to launch, the campaign that would destroy Damian's reputation and force Marcus into financial ruin and publicly humiliate Sarah for her security vulnerabilities. He saw it now not as an act of betrayal against his friends, but as a necessary intervention in a failing system. He was the architect. The system was compromised. The corrupted modules had to be isolated, not out of malice, but out of architectural necessity.

Chris's words had transformed the way Aaron understood his own power. He wasn't choosing to hurt his friends. He was choosing to protect the system that his friends had agreed to be part of. If they'd been true architects themselves, if they'd shared the vision of what Ethos Tech was supposed to be, they wouldn't be in this position. The fact that they'd been seduced by Damian's spreadsheet meant that they'd already abandoned the architectural principles. Aaron was just administering a correction.

He opened his eyes and looked at Chris. The driver was watching the road, an anchor of calm in the dark. The silver at his temples caught the passing streetlights, flickering like small signals in a code Aaron was only beginning to decipher. There was something almost timeless about Chris in this moment—not old, not young, not existing in any particular era Aaron could identify. Just present. Just aware.

"Take me back to the office," Aaron said, his voice quiet but steady—the voice of a man who had stopped bargaining with the universe and started negotiating with himself. "I have a war to prepare for. I have six hours to arrange the revelation of truths that the board doesn't yet know. I have to structure the information so that each fact lands like a hammer blow. I have to orchestrate the meeting so that Damian can't recover from the first shock before the second one hits him."

Chris glanced at him, and in that brief look, Aaron saw something he had never seen in his advisor's eyes before: not just approval, but relief. As if Chris had been waiting—patiently, across some distance Aaron could not measure—for this exact moment. As if the entire evening had been a test of Aaron's understanding, a final examination before the real work could begin.

"You understand now," Chris said. It wasn't a question.

"I understand," Aaron confirmed. "The fight is my duty. The outcome is not my responsibility. I execute the strategy with precision and detachment, and then I surrender to whatever the universe decides."

Chris smiled. It was the first genuine smile Aaron had seen from him—not the cryptic, knowing smile, but something warmer, something that suggested real pleasure at a student's learning.

The car accelerated silently through the empty streets, carrying the two men back toward the glass tower and the battle that waited inside it. The financial district was quiet at this hour—just the occasional taxi, the distant siren of an ambulance, the hum of the city's nocturnal machinery. Somewhere down there, Damian was probably awake, reviewing his own strategy, checking his own weapons, calculating the odds. Somewhere down there, Sarah and Marcus were sleeping, unaware that the person they'd betrayed had decided to strike back.

But Aaron was no longer thinking about them. He was thinking about the strategy. He was thinking about the sequence in which the information would be revealed. He was thinking about how to structure the confrontation so that truth would cut through the fog of lies like a laser through darkness.

They had work to do. And for the first time since the ambush in the boardroom, Aaron felt something close to excitement. Not the excitement of victory, but the excitement of a craftsperson being given the opportunity to execute the perfect design.
